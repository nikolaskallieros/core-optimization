## Function

Vacuum distillation managed coolant composition by separating material according to volatility. Together with makeup and withdrawal, it limited the accumulation of high-boiling degradation products. These high boilers were not simply filterable dirt: a substantial dissolved fraction was deliberately present in the coolant. Particulate filtration and high-boiler control therefore addressed different material populations. [AECL-2943, §§2.4–2.5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=9).

The 1967 discussion of an expected 30–40% equilibrium should not override the later 33% limit. These describe different stages of development and have different purposes.

## Vacuum equipment and commissioning

The purification plant was designed for 1 lbf/in² absolute, calculated as about 6.89 kPa absolute. Commissioning could not achieve operation at that pressure without air entering through valve-stem packing and packed pump shaft seals. The modifications included bellows-seal valves, improved pump seals and welded connections. [AECL-2552, §2.2](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=8).

This was consequential because oxygen ingress was associated with coolant fouling. Vacuum integrity was therefore part of maintaining coolant quality, not solely a separation-performance issue. The source reports the problem and corrective changes but does not publish a final leak rate, complete pressure/temperature operating envelope or distillation throughput.

## Material balance and makeup

Fresh-coolant addition compensated for material removed or lost; recovery and recycle reduced net demand. High-boiler cracking could return some material to lower-molecular-weight fractions, so gross production and net discard were different quantities. A useful balance would distinguish makeup, reusable distillate, residual high boilers, gases, volatile losses, sampling and leaks. This is a bookkeeping interpretation of the documented processes, not a reconstructed WR-1 mass balance.

The 1967 publication gave a projected purification cost of 0.028 mill/kWh(e) for a hypothetical 200-MWe station with stated financial assumptions. It is not a WR-1 cost per unit of thermal energy or a measured component operating expense. [AECL-2943, p. 6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=10).

## Evidence limits

Column dimensions, internals, reboiler duty, residence time, product cut points, throughput, actual recovery fractions and waste-disposal allocations were not recovered. Reactor coolant temperature cannot be substituted for distillation temperature, and 33% high boilers does not by itself establish acceptable chemistry or local fuel heat transfer. The controlled operating manuals and organic-coolant summary report would be needed to reconstruct the full process specification.
