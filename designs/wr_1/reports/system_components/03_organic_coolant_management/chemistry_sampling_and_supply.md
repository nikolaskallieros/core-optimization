## What was controlled

Coolant analysis supported three linked objectives: avoiding deposits on heated surfaces, maintaining the oxide barrier on zirconium alloys, and maintaining useful bulk properties as the mixture aged. Water was deliberately present at low concentration; simply making the organic as dry as possible would not reproduce the reported chemistry strategy. [AECL-2553, §3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=6).

## Mechanisms and control actions

Water helped preserve or repair the protective zirconium oxide film; chloride contamination could undermine that protection and promote hydrogen uptake. The 1966 account restricted chlorinated-solvent use, distinguished a 2-ppm fresh-feed chlorine specification from the lower achieved circulating concentration, and described a palladium-on-alumina chlorine-removal catalyst available when needed. This does not establish continuous catalyst use throughout reactor life. [AECL-2553, §3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=6).

Oxygen ingress was another concern. It could arise during opening of channels or through vacuum-purification seals. The documented remedies included piping changes during commissioning and improved vacuum boundary hardware. Chemistry management therefore depended on mechanical configuration as well as sample results. [AECL-2552, §2.2](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=7).

## Temperature and remaining evidence

HB-40's low melting point reduced trace-heating requirements, but the later operating account still identified standby-pump seals as a trace-heating concern. A low-melting fresh fluid therefore did not remove all local cold-state issues, especially as high boilers accumulated. [AECL-3523, §7](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=13).

No single source provides a lifetime chemical specification covering every coolant batch, material, fuel experiment and temperature. Nor do the retained passages establish full relief-system capacity or gas-service reliability. The appropriate interpretation is a set of dated chemical-control practices with demonstrated importance, not a complete transferable operating procedure.
