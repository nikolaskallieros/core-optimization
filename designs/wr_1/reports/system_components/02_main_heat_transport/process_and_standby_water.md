## Heat-rejection arrangement

The main exchangers transferred organic-coolant heat to river water. Water also cooled experimental loops, auxiliary equipment, pumps and motors, and supplied building services. The process-water and standby-water systems served different functions but used the site river-water infrastructure. Their names alone do not establish fully independent intake or power arrangements. [CNL 2022, §3.2.2.1](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=149).

| Component | Documented arrangement |
| --- | --- |
| Site pump house | Process-water pumps drew from the Winnipeg River supply system. |
| Underground supply | 24-in-diameter concrete pipe, calculated as about 0.610 m diameter. |
| Building inlet | Strainer followed by a pressure-control station. |
| Downstream branches | Process water for ordinary cooling; standby water for essential components unable to tolerate loss of process-water cooling. |
| Return | Process drainage carried water to the river via the outfall. |
| Organic trap | Intended to separate entrained organic material before discharge. |

Source: [CNL 2020, §2.10](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=12). The trap's existence does not quantify removal efficiency or guarantee containment of dissolved, emulsified or bypassed material.

## Exchanger boundary and operating experience

The later description assigns roughly 20 MWth to each main circuit and identifies conventional shell-and-tube exchangers. The reviewed passages do not identify a complete tube count, area, fouling allowance, water flow, temperature approach or pump head. The rated duty is consequently insufficient to reconstruct a heat-transfer coefficient or summer river-temperature margin. [CNL 2020, §2.4](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=8).

| Event or observation | Scope of the evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Two organic-side flange-gasket leaks in the early account | Exchangers were removed and repaired; the author reported no contamination on those particular repairs. |
| Two internal main-exchanger leaks in 1978 | The 1980 review attributes them to secondary-water corrosion and reports no corresponding organic-side pitting or fouling. |
| Approximately 300 L reached the river through a pinhole exchanger leak in August 1967 | Retrospective fact-sheet account; the underlying incident report was not reviewed. |

Sources: [AECL-3523, p. 9](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=14); [AECL-6436, §3.2.6](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=44); [2020 fact sheet, p. 2](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/135531E.pdf#page=2).

The 1978 findings show that favourable organic-side corrosion behaviour did not eliminate exchanger integrity problems. The 1967 release shows that an exchanger breach could connect the organic inventory to the discharge system. Neither observation establishes leak direction under every pressure condition.

## Interfaces and evidence limits

Standby water served essential cooling duties, including some loop condenser and support-system equipment described in the EIS. Moderator cooling also interacted with reactivity: commissioning added a trip for excessive moderator/process-water temperature difference after rapid cooling raised moderator level. Thus more aggressive cooling was not automatically benign for all connected systems. [CNL 2022, p. 3-8](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=148); [AECL-2552, §2.1](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=6).

Water-flow allocation, isolation logic, strainer differential-pressure limits, standby transfer times and ultimate heat-sink acceptance conditions were not recovered. Later decommissioning descriptions of drainage treatment must also be dated separately from the original operating discharge arrangements. No present-day discharge compliance conclusion is made here.
