## Handling arrangement

Fuel strings were withdrawn during shutdown through channel top closures. Rotating deck plates provided access, and the reactor-hall crane handled the large shielded flask. CNL describes removal roughly six hours after shutdown, with fuel remaining immersed in organic during initial transfer to the storage block. That timing is a historical description rather than a general minimum cooling time. [AECL-3523, p. 3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=8); [CNL 2020, §2.8](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=11).

| --- | --- | --- |
| Large fuel transfer flask | Approximately 10.6 m high, 1.24 m diameter at its base, 41 Mg; shield body, lifting spider, inner vessel, mechanisms and cooling | Also called the 45-ton flask in the 2020 description; source unit conventions are retained. |
| Fuel-channel transfer flask | Similar general form for irradiated channels/inserts not requiring cooling | Manual cable drives; EIS explicitly states no instrumentation/interlocks, cooling or ventilation. |
| Smaller hot-cell transfer flask | Approximately 19 Mg; lead-shielded stainless pipe 8.1 m long | Also described as the 21-ton flask in the 2020 account. |

Sources: [CNL 2022, pp. 3-13–3-14](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=153); [CNL 2020, p. 11](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=11). These are not interchangeable load envelopes.

## Large fuel-flask internals and services

The inner vessel was a water-jacketed stainless-steel tube with a lower ball valve and snout. A yoke and two screws moved the vessel vertically. Outer shielding supported the equipment and attenuated radiation from the fuel. During fuelling, a flexible connection joined the stationary tube to the building active-ventilation pit exhaust to remove released organic or radioactive gas. [CNL 2022, p. 3-13](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=153).

Its cooling circuit used a stainless water tank, circulation pump, connecting lines and instrumentation. Steam connections allowed heating where required; an emergency once-through process-water cooling arrangement was also described. The external water jacket did not mean that the fuel was normally transferred in direct contact with that water. [Same source](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=153).

## Station support and interlocks

| Interface | Stated provision |
| --- | --- |
| Storage block access | Heavily shielded door; flask interlocks prevented block fuelling unless the door was locked shut. |
| Spent-fuel station | Recessed spigot plate positioned the flask and supported the transfer interface. |
| Receiving trolley | Interlock prevented lowering fuel unless the trolley was positioned to receive it. |
| Dripping organic | Guide tube and drip tray contained leakage from the inner vessel at the transfer station. |

Source: [CNL 2022, p. 3-11](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=151). These are selected identified interlocks, not a complete permissive matrix.

## Maintenance and evidence limits

The EIS describes a flask-maintenance station with a 12-m-deep shaft, approximately 2.1 m by 1.8 m, a lower platform and mechanisms for removing shutter/inner-vessel assemblies. Its presence supports the maintainability of the hardware but supplies no inspection intervals or allowable wear. [CNL 2022, p. 3-14](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=154).

Missing information includes crane capacity and load combinations, flask centre of gravity, cooling duty, maximum fuel decay heat, permitted transfer duration, seal qualification and drop/criticality analyses. The channel flask's explicit absence of cooling and interlocks is particularly important when comparing equipment: features of the large fuel flask cannot be assumed for the channel flask.
