## Process and active drainage are different paths

Process drainage returned used process/standby cooling water to the Winnipeg River through the outfall. An organic trap was intended to separate organic entrained in that water. Active drainage instead collected potentially contaminated liquids from rooms and equipment areas, with separate routes for general, organic and heavy-water drainage. [CNL 2020, §2.10](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=12).

A main-exchanger leak could therefore connect organic coolant to the process-water discharge path without first following the floor-drain network. The documented trap is a mitigation component, not proof that every organic release was retained.

## Collection and transfer details

The EIS describes predominantly welded seamless carbon-steel active-drain piping. Floor traps included backwater valves serving as ventilation barriers. Moderator/boron/demineralizer room drains went to the heavy-water sump, with level monitoring and remote sampling; liquid could go to general active drainage or a heavy-water drumming station. Organic-room drains led to the organic sump, which could transfer to Active Drainage A. [CNL 2022, p. 3-15](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=155).

These paths preserved some source segregation while still permitting downstream transfer. They also coupled liquid handling to airborne-contamination control through the drain traps. The public descriptions do not provide the complete piping elevations, overflow routes or valve-state history needed for a spill-routing calculation.

## Liquid treatment and time scope

In the 2022 description, Active Drainage A discharged to low-level liquid-waste treatment comprising two high-density polyethylene tanks, a sampling station and two filtering units. Effluent was held, sampled, analysed, filtered and adjusted before release against applicable criteria. The passage does not publish those criteria or a removal-efficiency curve. [CNL 2022, p. 3-15](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=155).

Subsurface water was normally routed to stormwater management, with an option for liquid-waste treatment following an external spill. This is the later described configuration; installation dates and historical modifications were not established. It must not be assumed to have been unchanged throughout 1965–1985.

## Contamination and unresolved performance

Fuel failures contaminated primary coolant and auxiliaries, so spent filters, clay, tank sludge and drained organic could carry activity even where normal coolant corrosion activity was low. CNL explicitly identifies these retained contamination pathways. [CNL 2020, §§3.1.4, 6.3](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=23).

Trap efficiency for emulsified/dissolved organics, treatment throughput, historical discharge permissions, sump operating levels and event-specific inventories remain unknown. No claim of zero release, complete radionuclide removal or present compliance follows from the equipment list. Reconstructing an incident requires its actual drainage configuration and monitoring records.
