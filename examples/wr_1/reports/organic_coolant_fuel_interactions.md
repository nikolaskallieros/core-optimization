## 1. Main assessment

**Coolant evolution and coolant–fuel interactions are central uncertainties for transferring organic cooling to a new design, but the historical evidence is much stronger than “we do not know what irradiation does to the coolant.”** Researchers measured decomposition, gas and high-boiler production, changing properties, fouling, hydriding and several kinds of fuel failure. The unresolved problem is predicting their combined effect for a specified coolant, fuel, cladding, temperature history, radiation field and cleanup system. The recovered evidence does not establish that this is the largest uncertainty for every possible organic-cooled reactor. [R1, PDF pp. 3–7](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=3); [R4, abstract and synthesis](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=5); [R11, pp. 45–52](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=52).

| Question | What the evidence establishes | What remains unresolved for a new application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does radiation change the coolant? | Yes: parent compounds are consumed and gases, lighter products, heavier soluble products and sometimes insoluble material form. | Product distributions and rates for the chosen fluid under its actual neutron/gamma, temperature and processing history. |
| Does degradation inevitably produce unacceptable fuel deposits? | No. Controlled irradiated mixtures supported useful heat transfer; other conditions produced serious deposits and blockage. | Local deposition and removal rates, including impurity excursions and poorly cooled regions. |
| Can zirconium cladding survive? | Yes, in specific historical chemistry conditions. Water content, chlorine, alloy and surface condition strongly affected hydrogen uptake. | Long-term uptake, redistribution and mechanical consequences in the selected cladding and support geometry. |
| Is a small fuel breach benign? | Sometimes activity stayed low; other tests showed internal coke, swelling, splitting and progressive restriction. | Defect growth and release rates as functions of fuel form, breach geometry, power cycling and coolant condition. |
| Does low normal coolant activity bound a failure? | No. WR-1 experienced persistent circuit contamination and loss of failed-fuel-monitor usefulness after an experimental fuel rupture. | Nuclide-specific transport, deposition, cleanup and detector response for the selected failure scenario. |

The most useful distinction is between **bulk circulating coolant**, **coolant next to the heated cladding surface**, and **coolant trapped inside a breached fuel element**. Measurements of the first cannot, by themselves, determine the condition of the other two. Sections 4–7 develop the evidence behind these conclusions.

## 2. What changes as the coolant is irradiated

### 2.1 The coolant becomes a changing mixture

HB-40 was a mixture of partially hydrogenated terphenyls. Santowax OM was principally ortho- and meta-terphenyl; the MIT WR/OMP mixtures were meta-rich. Dowtherm A contains biphenyl and diphenyl ether. These fluids have different starting chemistry, and evidence from one should not be assigned to another solely because all are called organic coolants. [R1, PDF pp. 3–5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=3); [R4, Chapter 1](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=24); [R14, methods](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8b99d0bc-e31b-4040-a5d3-8838bfc30e8a/content#page=4).

| Product or change | Source-supported behavior | Consequence for coolant and fuel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gas and volatile products | Historical polyphenyl systems produced hydrogen and light hydrocarbons. HB-40 generated more gas and benzene-range volatile material than Santowax OM in the cited comparison. | Gas removal and volatile inventory affect pressure/composition; dissolved hydrogen is relevant to exposed zirconium surfaces. |
| Low and intermediate boilers | Some products remain in the liquid or recycle with the distillate. HB-40-derived volatiles could undergo further irradiation reactions. | Processing changes the subsequent chemistry; a single-pass decomposition yield does not define the long-term mixture. |
| High boilers, abbreviated HB | The fraction contains a broad mixture of higher-molecular-weight material. Characterization found polyphenyl structures, fused rings, alkyl groups and unsaturation. | HB is a measured fraction, not one chemical species with one viscosity, thermal stability or deposit-forming tendency. |
| Insoluble material | Irradiated HB contained benzene-insoluble material; heating could increase this fraction and redistribute chlorine into heavy fractions. | Distinguish dissolved heavy molecules from suspended solids and adherent deposits. They require different measurements and removal processes. |
| Hydrogenation and molecular structure | HB-40 decomposition products could crack at elevated temperature into lighter material; some remained useful coolant. | Parent-fluid disappearance, HB production and fresh-makeup consumption are different quantities. |

Sources: [R1, pp. 3–4/PDF pp. 4–5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=4); [R5, summary and characterization](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4443349#page=7); [R13, OMRE coolant description, p. 10](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4160581#page=18).

A darkened fluid, a higher HB percentage or a change in average molecular weight therefore identifies a change without uniquely identifying the thermal-hydraulic consequence. IDO-16909 found that high-molecular-weight fractions formed more film in its laboratory glass/benzene tests, but explicitly treated correlation with reactor fouling as further work. Those tests should not be converted into a fuel-surface deposition law. [R5, summary](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4443349#page=7).

### 2.2 Radiation damage and thermal damage are coupled through composition

The MIT experiments distinguished radiolysis from *radiopyrolysis*: thermal decomposition of coolant whose composition had already been changed by irradiation. Irradiated Santowax decomposed thermally faster than virgin fluid in the reported autoclave experiments. Thus, coolant can continue degrading while it resides in hot equipment outside the core. [R4, abstract and pp. 1.28–1.34](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=50).

The historical analysis represented total degradation as the sum of radiolysis and radiopyrolysis. This was an empirical model with assumptions: the high-temperature radiolysis term was extrapolated from lower-temperature data and subtracted from measured total degradation. The inferred thermal term consequently had large standard errors—about **90% at 370°C, 25% at 400°C and 10% at 430°C**. These are source-reported uncertainties in that inferred rate, not uncertainty bounds on reactor lifetime or fuel-failure probability. [R4, pp. 1.21 and 1.34](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=43).

The same study found an apparent radiolysis reaction order of **1.7 ± 0.1** for Santowax OM at 300°C and estimated a fast-neutron/gamma effectiveness ratio of about **3.3**. These describe the particular measured mixtures and model. They are not universal factors for converting modern gamma-vial results into reactor damage. Pure-isomer capsule experiments also behaved differently from maintained mixtures. [R4, abstract and pp. 1.34–1.35](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=5).

### 2.3 The operating coolant includes its treatment history

The MIT loop maintained composition by withdrawing coolant, separating HB, and returning distillate plus fresh makeup. Low and intermediate boilers remained in the recycle. The relevant exposure therefore includes residence time, hot inventory, dose distribution and removal rates; elapsed reactor time or total dose alone is insufficient. [R4, abstract and operating procedure](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=5).

Treatment can itself change the chemistry. When X-7 switched to HB-40, its palladium/alumina–magnesium chlorine-removal bed was withdrawn because palladium on alumina efficiently dehydrogenated HB-40 above 200°C, producing poorly soluble para-terphenyl among other products. A cleanup arrangement suitable for one coolant could therefore create a new composition problem with another. [R3, §2.4, p. 3/PDF p. 8](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4235504#page=8).

## 3. Quantitative evidence for coolant evolution

### 3.1 HB-40 and Santowax OM under comparable mixed-field exposure

The E-2 comparison was performed at **350°C**, with **33 wt% HB**, a test-section dose rate of about **0.3 W/g**, and **62% of absorbed energy attributed to fast-neutron interactions**. The rate denominator is radiation energy **absorbed in the coolant**, not reactor thermal energy. [R1, Table II, p. 3/PDF p. 4](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=4).

| Reported rate, g per kWh absorbed in coolant | Santowax OM | HB-40 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Gas formation | <0.2 | 0.7 |
| Volatile formation | 0.0 | 2.4 |
| HB formation | 16.5 | 15.0 |
| Total coolant consumption | 16.5 | 18.1 |

The measured consumption values differ by about **10%**, while the product distribution differs substantially. The original table gives **16.5** for Santowax HB formation; a machine text extraction can misread this as 10.5. The table and subsequent discussion distinguish net behavior from the further cracking and recombination of products. Do not treat these rounded entries as a universal, complete reaction mass balance. [R1, Table II and following discussion](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=4).

### 3.2 High-boiler concentration and its measurement

In the HB-40 X-7 campaign, the initial charge measured **2.6% HB**, rising to **4.2%** after commissioning operation. Makeup subsequently affected the measured concentration. The reported mean production was **2.7 kg HB per MWd of loop power**, with individual test-section periods giving 2.2–2.9 kg/MWd. This power-normalized rate cannot be compared directly with the E-2 radiation-energy-normalized rates. [R3, §3.7 and Table I, p. 5/PDF p. 10](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4235504#page=10).

A change in the HB assay produced **13.4% instead of 10.0% for the same coolant basis**: an absolute shift of 3.4 percentage points. The report corrected its later values when estimating production. This is a consequential warning for data compilation: the distillation protocol belongs with every HB value. An apparent change in degradation can be an analytical-method change. [R3, §3.7](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4235504#page=10).

### 3.3 Properties change, but not always in the same direction

| Evidence | Reported result | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Historical HB-40 comparison at 350°C | Viscosity increased from about **0.29 to 0.47 cP** between HB-40 and HB-40 containing 30% HB; density increased from **0.76 to 0.80 g/cm³**. | Degraded-mixture properties should enter hydraulic and heat-transfer calculations. This comparison is not a time-resolved universal aging curve. |
| Same source, vapor pressure at 350°C | HB-40: **19 psia**; HB-40 + 30% HB: **20–50 psia**, depending on degassing and low-boiler content. | Removing or retaining light products can matter more than a nominal HB percentage alone. |
| MIT mixed-field Santowax WR, new TH8 heater | Heat transfer followed **Nu = 0.023 Re^0.8 Pr^0.4 within approximately ±10%**, over 630–800°F, 8–20.5% HB, Re ≈ 3×10⁴–1.3×10⁵ and Pr ≈ 5.2–8.2. | Conventional turbulent correlations could describe selected clean-surface data when appropriate mixture properties were used. |
| MIT Santowax OM, previously used TH7 heater | Measured heat-transfer coefficient fell to about **70%** of the correlation prediction. | Actual deposit resistance must be treated separately from bulk-fluid property changes. |

Sources: [R1, Table I](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=4); [R4, p. 1.39 and pp. 3.26–3.27](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8338dd6b-c0e8-48bb-98a3-e61c2e4682d1/content#page=61). The MIT authors suspected impurity introduction, possibly oxygen exposure, during modifications; they did not chemically characterize the TH7 deposit. Changing heater and coolant together does not isolate coolant identity as the cause.

The modern Dowtherm A study provides a useful counterexample to “irradiation always increases viscosity.” Static samples received **1 MGy of Co-60 gamma exposure**, under air or argon at room temperature or argon at 250°C. Room-temperature irradiated specimens had lower viscosity; the hot-argon specimen was closer to the control. Larger molecules were detected, but high-boiling yields were not fully quantified. The Pyrex-contained samples did not test heated cladding contact, flowing cleanup or neutron damage. These measurements cannot supply a WR-1 property or corrosion correlation. [R14, methods and results, PDF pp. 4–5 and 9–11](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/8b99d0bc-e31b-4040-a5d3-8838bfc30e8a/content#page=4).

## 4. How coolant affects the outside of the fuel cladding

### 4.1 Fouling has several mechanisms

Charlesworth distinguished deposits formed by transport of dissolved iron-containing material from deposits formed by attachment of insoluble organic material. The resulting films could contain both organic and inorganic constituents. Higher surface temperature promoted both mechanisms, but increasing velocity affected them differently: it increased mass-transfer deposition and decreased particulate deposition in the studied conditions. A single “more flow always means less deposition” rule is therefore inadequate. [R6, conclusions 1–4, pp. 81–82/PDF pp. 85–86](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4631967#page=85).

The same report judged direct nuclear irradiation of the deposition process to be a secondary influence under its representative operating conditions. That does not contradict irradiation-driven formation of degradation products, and it does not apply automatically to stagnant coolant or organic in direct contact with exposed fissioning fuel. **Product generation and deposition of those products are separate steps.** [R6, conclusion 5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4631967#page=86); [R9, §5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4797456#page=25).

A particularly useful X-7 result involved SAP-clad UO₂ trefoils irradiated to a maximum **2,400 MWd/tU**. The report describes approximately **80 μm** of deposit, comprising **40 wt% polymerized organic and 60 wt% Fe₃O₄**, accompanied by a **50% pressure-drop increase** and a **60°C sheath-temperature rise**. The source gives heat flux near **100 W/cm² = 1.00 MW/m²**. This demonstrates a significant thermal and hydraulic penalty without requiring a massive visible coke obstruction. [R8, synopsis, PDF p. 3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4690564#page=3).

### 4.2 Deposition can amplify local overheating

For interpretation, a thin deposit can be represented by the approximate local relation

$$
T_{\mathrm{clad,outer}}-T_{\mathrm{bulk}}
\approx q''\left(\frac{1}{h}+R_f\right),
\qquad R_f\approx\frac{\delta_f}{k_f}.
$$

Here q″ is local heat flux in W/m², h is the coolant-side heat-transfer coefficient in W/(m²·K), and R_f is the area-normalized deposit resistance in m²·K/W; δ_f and k_f are deposit thickness in m and effective conductivity in W/(m·K). This is an engineering interpretation, not a fitted organic-fouling model. Real deposits also change flow area, roughness and local distribution; a measured deposit thickness alone does not establish its resistance.

The potentially reinforcing chain is: deposition increases thermal resistance or restricts flow; the cladding surface gets hotter; temperature-sensitive deposition can increase further. Its magnitude depends on local geometry, deposit composition and removal. The historical evidence supports individual steps and observed deterioration, but this review has not calculated a coupled feedback growth rate.

Later WR-1 tests show the importance of local flow. AECL-6436 reports long operation at **7.5 m/s** without increased fouling, but increased deposition and some premature fuel failures at average velocities of **3.0 and 4.8 m/s**. The authors' rejection of velocities below about 5 m/s was explicitly tied to the standard cluster and subchannels of uncertain velocity. It is not a transferable minimum for another bundle. [R11, §4.2.2.2, p. 51/PDF p. 58](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=58).

### 4.3 Particles produced elsewhere can damage the fuel

In OMRE, the inlet passages of experimental aluminum-clad uranium-alloy fuel elements became substantially blocked by material filtered from the coolant. Local overheating melted cladding on one element and exposed the uranium alloy. The hottest damaged locations were not instrumented by the installed thermocouples. The report found no evidence of chemical reaction between the coolant and the exposed core alloy or melted aluminum–uranium material. [R13, pp. 36–37/PDF pp. 44–45](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4160581#page=44).

This is evidence of **coolant-borne blockage causing fuel damage** without evidence that direct coolant–fuel chemical attack caused that damage. Neither result can be generalized into universal compatibility of exposed uranium metal with irradiated organic fluid.

## 5. Cladding compatibility: chemistry, alloy and temperature all matter

### 5.1 Zirconium alloys: an oxide barrier controls hydrogen entry

Early tests sometimes found rapid, severe hydriding of zirconium alloys in organic coolant. Canadian work demonstrated much better behavior when the protective oxide was maintained and chlorine contamination controlled. In Santowax OM tests at 365–425°C, bare specimens in very dry coolant—**below about 50 ppm water**—could absorb large amounts of hydrogen; preoxidation helped. With sufficient water, both initially pickled and preoxidized specimens developed much lower uptake. [R7, p. 3/PDF p. 6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=6).

Water was therefore deliberately controlled, rather than simply minimized. Historical values changed with source and operating period:

| Source/context | Water concentration reported | Chlorine condition reported |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AECL-2640 long-duration material tests | 80–150 ppm | Generally <1 ppm; brief excursions occurred. |
| WR-1 specification quoted in that 1966 report | 100–200 ppm | <2 ppm. |
| X-7 HB-40 campaign, 1965–1966 | 50–150 ppm | <0.5 ppm chemical-analysis detection limit. |
| WR-1 operating account, 1969 manuscript | 50–500 ppm discussed for chemistry control | Chloride contamination to remain below 0.5 ppm. |

Sources: [R7, p. 4](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=7); [R3, p. 4](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4235504#page=9); [R10, p. 8](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=13). These are dated experimental conditions and operating statements, **not a single recommended chemistry window for a new reactor**. A nondetect also means below the stated detection limit, not zero chlorine.

Chlorine was especially damaging. In short tests with low-nickel Zircaloy-2, **10 ppm chlorine added as HCl**, coolant at 400°C and water below 60 ppm, preoxidized samples reportedly absorbed hydrogen at about **1 μg/(cm²·h) at 400°C** and **100 μg/(cm²·h) at 480°C**. Chemical form mattered: trichloroethylene and chlorobiphenyl did not have the same effect. The authors did not establish a universally safe chlorine threshold or a complete microscopic mechanism. [R7, p. 4/PDF p. 7](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=7).

### 5.2 Bulk hydrogen concentration does not define local embrittlement

Hydrogen uptake differed between alloys, and hydrogen could migrate from hot cladding to colder attached parts. The X-719 examinations reported up to **9,000 ppm hydrogen in a Zircaloy-2 wire wrap**, versus roughly **200–500 ppm in its cladding**. The wrap on a Zr–2.5Nb-clad element contained about **1,500 ppm**, with cladding at **120–170 ppm**. The authors attributed much of this localization to thermal migration. [R7, p. 5/PDF p. 8](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=8).

Later WR-1 experience included hydrogen migration into bundle end plates, causing embrittlement and breakage during handling; bundle-design changes addressed that problem. A hydrogen sink can protect one location while becoming a vulnerable component itself. A life assessment must preserve temperature distribution, material continuity, cooling/handling history and the location where mechanical integrity matters. [R11, p. 47/PDF p. 54](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=54).

The older tests did not establish one irradiation multiplier for hydriding. They suggested an increase around 350°C in one pressure-tube exposure, while higher-temperature fuel-cladding data did not show the same additional effect. Early service-life estimates also depended on assumed hydrogen tolerances. Those estimates should remain historical forecasts rather than be reused as qualified modern lifetimes. [R7, pp. 5–6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=8).

### 5.3 SAP and stainless steel have different limitations

SAP—sintered aluminum product—showed good corrosion resistance in the reviewed organic coolants, but low high-temperature ductility and variable fabrication quality constrained fuel use. SAP/UO₂ failures could involve pellet cracking, cycling and mechanical strain even where general coolant corrosion was low. The reported improvements with stronger SAP and lower sheath temperature were conditional results, not proof that chemistry was irrelevant. [R7, pp. 1–3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=4).

For stainless steel, AECL-2640 describes transgranular cracking after about **50 ppm chlorine as trichloroethylene** was added to an HB-40 loop. Failures occurred in colder stagnant or intermittently flowing branches; flowing specimens and other loops did not show identical behavior. This is an adjacent-system interaction: nominally compatible piping can introduce contamination or lose integrity under local conditions not represented by a well-flowing fuel-channel test. [R7, §3, p. 8/PDF p. 11](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4442652#page=11).

## 6. Fuel material matters even before the cladding fails

With intact cladding, the bulk organic does not directly wash the fuel. Its effects pass through cladding temperature, surface chemistry, heat transfer and system pressure; fuel swelling and fission-gas release then affect the internal gap and cladding loads.

| Fuel configuration | Relevant evidence | What should not be inferred |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UO₂ with zirconium-alloy cladding in WR-1 | The later review associated lower coolant pressure and higher cladding temperature with less restraint on swelling. Higher fuel temperature promoted fission-gas release; changing gap pressure and separation could reduce fuel-to-cladding heat transfer and intensify hot spots. | A failure in organic coolant does not automatically identify chemical attack by the organic as its initiating cause. |
| UC with zirconium-alloy cladding in WR-1 | UC's better thermal conductivity and lower operating fuel temperature supported lower fission-gas release and better reported performance. The 1980 account described more than 400 UC bundles irradiated and two defects to that date. | This dated count is not a lifetime failure probability. Hydriding, fouling and dimensional change still constrained performance. |
| Mixed NRU-305 experimental string | Four obvious defects were found in Zircaloy/UO₂ rods; the report separately examined the SAP/UC bundles. | Successful SAP/UC examination cannot be assigned to the failed UO₂ rods, or treated as a controlled one-variable comparison. |
| Aluminum-clad uranium-alloy experimental elements in OMRE | Inlet blockage and local cladding melting were observed. | The alloy, geometry and failure route are distinct from WR-1 UO₂ and UC bundles. |
| Uranium-impregnated graphite experiment in WR-1 | Eight ruptured elements contaminated one primary circuit. | This experimental fuel event is not representative evidence for the release fraction of ordinary UC driver defects. |

Sources: [R11, pp. 45–48](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=52); [R12, abstract/PDF p. 9 and operating history/PDF p. 17](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4567371#page=17); [R13, pp. 36–37](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4160581#page=44); [R10, pp. 10–11](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=15).

## 7. What changes after a fuel breach

### 7.1 Coolant inside the element experiences a different environment

After a through-wall breach, organic can enter the fuel–cladding gap and fuel cracks. It may become hotter and much less mobile than the bulk coolant. Exposed fissioning fuel also introduces a local fission-fragment irradiation route that an intact cladding barrier normally excludes from coolant contact.

AECL-1390 observed a thick hard organic layer inside an intentionally defected SAP/UO₂ specimen, even though gross bulk coolant composition did not change markedly. The authors considered both local pyrolysis and fission-fragment-driven radiolysis important. Their numerical film-growth estimates were rough calculations using additional experiments, not direct measurements of elementary reaction rates in the fuel gap. [R9, §5, pp. 18–20/PDF pp. 25–27](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4797456#page=25).

| Post-breach interaction | Evidence and qualification | Possible consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organic enters gap/cracks and forms hard deposits | Direct deposit observation in AECL-1390. Repeated entry and decomposition during power cycles was an author-proposed explanation. | Space available for differential expansion changes; deposits can load the sheath and contribute to splitting. |
| Trapped coolant heats, expands or vaporizes | Activity peaks on power changes supported an organic-logging interpretation. The cause of initial cracking was not uniquely established. | Transient pressure and expulsion of coolant/fission products through the breach. |
| Entered organic decomposes beside the inner zirconium surface | AECL-6436 explicitly attributes progressive failed-fuel deterioration to internal hydriding from decomposition of coolant entering through the defect. | The small breach may progress to mechanically damaged fuel and more difficult removal. |
| Deposits retain some material but also impede cooling | AECL-1390 found cracked pellets cemented by organic and little evidence of UO₂ particulate escape in that test. | Lower observed particulate release can coexist with worsening internal mechanical damage. |
| Deposits or debris enter circulation | AECL-1390 observed filter plugging; the authors proposed flaking organic deposits as its source. | Increased cleanup load and a potential path to downstream restriction; the particle source attribution remained an interpretation. |

Sources: [R9, §§5–8](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4797456#page=25); [R11, §3.3.5, p. 47](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=54).

### 7.2 The deliberate-defect UO₂ test demonstrates progression, not a universal clock

In the X-7 test, pressure drop first rose to roughly **twice** its initial value and then to **more than five times** after shutdown/restart. Examination found badly split sheathing and the gap filled with hard black organic. The authors could not assign a unique cause; substandard SAP, thermal cycling, coolant logging and deposit buildup were relevant. Their discussion reports deposits up to about **1 mm**, but different sections give different thickness ranges, so no single measured maximum is adopted here. [R9, summary and §§7–8, PDF pp. 3 and 29–33](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4797456#page=3).

The authors' tentative **40–60 h** removal discussion applied to this single test and its cycling history. By contrast, AECL-6436 described serious internal-hydriding deterioration over several weeks in its failed-fuel assessment, and actual UC defects with low circuit activity were operated until the next scheduled shutdown. These statements concern different fuel/cladding configurations and evidence bases. They cannot be combined into one generic allowable time after an organic-cooled fuel breach. [R9, §8.3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4797456#page=33); [R11, pp. 47–48](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=54).

### 7.3 Fission-product release and circuit transport are separate questions

Material must first escape the fuel and breached sheath. It can then remain dissolved or suspended, enter the gas space, deposit on surfaces, or be removed by treatment. Decay and continuing release occur throughout. The following observations should remain separate:

| Observation | What it establishes | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliberate SAP/UO₂ defect in X-7 | Gas release was comparable in order to the cited water-loop defects; radioiodine release was lower. The authors proposed retention in the organic film. | Gas-space loss was not fully known; one test cannot establish an iodine retention factor for another fuel or fluid. |
| OMRE aluminum-clad uranium-alloy failure | Much of the released iodine stayed in the coolant; uranium was detected in the circulating inventory. | Primary-system inventory is not an environmental release, and this was not WR-1 UC fuel. |
| Later WR-1 UC defects | Signals were sufficient for detection while subsequent circuit activity remained low in the reported cases. | Low release does not prove no internal deterioration, and small signals place demands on detection. |
| WR-1 uranium-impregnated-graphite rupture | Gas purging left persistent piping contamination and high background. | Gas removal alone did not restore a clean circuit. |

Sources: [R9, §6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4797456#page=27); [R13, p. 16](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4160581#page=24); [R11, p. 48](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=55); [R10, pp. 10–11](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=15).

The severe WR-1 event is a strong counterexample to assuming that normally low organic-circuit activity guarantees easy recovery after failure. Eight uranium-impregnated-graphite elements ruptured, releasing gaseous fission products and some Ba/La-140. Piping fields reached **50 R/h after gas purging**. High background made the failed-fuel detection system inoperable; particulate removal assisted recovery, and operation resumed after about **six weeks**. These are historical exposure-rate readings and an event-specific recovery duration, not modern dose limits or a predicted cleanup time. [R10, pp. 10–11/PDF pp. 15–16](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=15).

### 7.4 Water ingress changes the problem for exposed UC

Trace water used to maintain a zirconium oxide film should not be conflated with substantial water contact after fuel exposure. AECL-6436 discusses irradiated UC hydrolysis under water cooling, producing principally methane and hydrogen and disintegrating the fuel. It also discusses radioactive-species retention and release in that accident context, while explicitly stating that the retention mechanism required further explanation and verification. Its quoted radioiodine release estimate is therefore not adopted here as a transferable source-term fraction. [R11, §3.3.3.2, p. 44/PDF p. 51](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=51).

The reviewed passages do not establish the rate of attack on exposed irradiated UC by a specified trace-water concentration in HB-40. That missing relation is distinct from both controlled cladding chemistry and a bulk-water accident. Direct coolant–fuel qualification must include the water state, available inventory and exposure conditions rather than borrowing either result unchanged.

## 8. Which interactions need to be represented together

| Source → target | Mechanism and relevant state | Evidence status | First missing quantitative connection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Radiation field → coolant composition | Parent-fluid destruction and product formation depend on radiation type, absorbed energy, composition and temperature. | Mixed-field experiments. | A validated rate/composition description for the selected fluid and spectrum. |
| Irradiated mixture → hot out-of-core coolant | Changed composition increases subsequent thermal decomposition. | MIT loop/autoclave evidence. | Inventory-weighted rates over the actual temperature and residence-time distribution. |
| Impurities/solids → cladding surface | Dissolved-material transport and particulate attachment produce different deposits. | Loop experiments and post-irradiation examinations. | Local deposition/removal rates and effective deposit conductivity. |
| Deposit/geometry → cooling → further deposition | Added resistance and restriction raise surface temperature and can change local flow. | Measured consequences; feedback interpretation. | Coupled evolution and onset of sustained deterioration in the actual bundle. |
| Water/chlorine/hydrogen → zirconium → supports | Oxide condition governs entry; temperature gradients redistribute absorbed hydrogen. | Materials tests and WR-1 handling experience. | Lifetime and integrity at the mechanically limiting location and operating phase. |
| Breach → internal organic → fuel/cladding | Confined decomposition, hard deposits, logging and internal hydriding. | Direct observations plus qualified author mechanisms. | Defect-growth kinetics for the specified fuel, alloy, gap and power history. |
| Failed fuel → coolant/gas/surfaces → monitoring | Release, partitioning, deposition and removal set measured activity and detector background. | Operating events and experimental release measurements. | Nuclide-specific inventories, transfer rates and detection margins. |
| Cleanup equipment → mixture and radioactive inventory | Distillation, adsorption, filtration and degassing remove different fractions; some treatment surfaces also alter coolant chemistry. | Operating equipment and chemistry evidence. | Treatment performance for aged and contaminated fluid, including secondary-waste loading. |

This table is a synthesis of R1–R13, with the detailed evidence and qualifications in Sections 2–7. It identifies model scope; it does not claim that a coupled calculation has been performed or that every connection has a known magnitude.

The treatment functions are also distinct in the historical hardware. X-7 used clay adsorption with downstream woven-glass filtration to retain clay, followed by sintered-steel backup filters. This arrangement addressed both removal of contaminants and prevention of treatment media entering the loop; a single overall “purification efficiency” would conceal those different functions. [R2, §3.2, p. 4/PDF p. 10](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4226713#page=10).

## 9. What is still unknown, and what evidence would resolve it

For a new design, the first missing input is the actual combination of coolant, fuel and cladding. Without that choice, an absolute ranking of all uncertainties would be speculative. The following order reflects dependencies in the present review, not numerical risk scores.

| Question to resolve | Existing basis | Most informative additional evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What mixture will circulate after sustained service? | Historical product and rate measurements; processing-dependent steady mixtures. | A closed material balance for the selected fluid, including gases, volatile liquid, soluble heavy fractions and insolubles under representative radiation and thermal history. |
| Does that mixture maintain local heat removal? | Clean-surface correlations coexist with fouled-heater and restricted-bundle failures. | Flowing heated-surface evidence with matched cladding, impurities and local flow; measured deposit composition, thermal resistance and hydraulic effects. |
| What controls cladding life? | Strong water/chlorine/alloy dependence and spatial hydrogen migration. | Matched irradiated material data and location-specific hydrogen/oxide examination, including cooling and handling conditions. |
| How does a small breach progress? | One detailed early SAP/UO₂ experiment and later zirconium/UC experience with different outcomes. | Defected-fuel evidence for the actual fuel/alloy/coolant combination, resolving internal chemistry, cycling effects and breach growth. |
| Where do released radionuclides go? | Event-specific gas, liquid and deposited activity observations. | Species-resolved balances across fuel, coolant, gas spaces, surfaces and treatment equipment; account for continuing release and decay. |
| What happens if water reaches exposed carbide? | Bulk-water hydrolysis was recognized; the review retained explicit uncertainties in release retention. | Applicable measurements for water dissolved in organic versus separate liquid/steam contact with the chosen irradiated carbide. |

Several useful historical originals remain missing from the recovered collection, including **AECL-4922, *Organic Coolant Summary Report***, and detailed underlying WR-1 materials/failed-fuel records. Their absence limits reconstruction of long-term chemistry histories and the provenance of some later summary claims. It does not demonstrate that those investigations were never performed.

## 10. Evidence checks and interpretation limits

This is a selected-passage synthesis of **14 primary documents**, not a full audit of every page or source in the broader collection. Consequential tables and passages were checked against original PDF text or page images. The checks included the E-2 rate denominator and table entries, X-7 assay shift, deposit/pressure-drop observations, chlorine/hydrogen conditions, deliberate-defect conclusions, later WR-1 fuel experience and the modern gamma-test arrangement.

- The E-2 rate table was checked visually to resolve an OCR ambiguity; historical numerical values and inequality signs are retained.
- Fluid identity, alloy, fuel form, irradiation arrangement and operating period remain attached to each example.
- Assay-dependent HB values, bulk properties, local deposits and primary-system radioactivity are treated as different observables.
- Observed outcomes are separated from author-proposed mechanisms, design forecasts and the engineering interpretation in this report.
- No new reactor simulation, fuel-failure rate, chemistry operating limit or radionuclide release fraction was derived.

**The strongest supported conclusion is that coolant chemistry, local fuel cooling and fuel integrity must be assessed as a coupled problem.** Historical operation demonstrated that substantial coolant degradation could be managed, but also showed that local deposits, impurity-sensitive cladding behavior and coolant entry after a breach can defeat conclusions drawn from bulk coolant quality alone. The main remaining work is to make those connections quantitative for the chosen design.
