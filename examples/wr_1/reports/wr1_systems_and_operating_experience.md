## 1. What operated in Canada

**Whiteshell Reactor No. 1 (WR-1), at Pinawa, Manitoba, demonstrated sustained operation of an organic-cooled, heavy-water-moderated pressure-tube reactor.** It first became critical on 1 November 1965 and operated until May 1985. It was a research and irradiation facility with water-cooled heat exchangers and no turbine. Its record supports the feasibility of this particular reactor technology; it does not supply measured electricity-generation costs. [R1, p. 1](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4891392#page=4); [R2, p. 5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=9); [R7, §2.1](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=7).

The main engineering trade was high coolant temperature at relatively low pressure, accompanied by a substantial coolant-treatment system. Near the fuel, performance depended on local flow, deposit formation and hydrogen uptake by zirconium alloys. Low normal coolant activity made maintenance easier, but experimental fuel failures could remove that advantage. The following report separates design values, observed operation and later retrospective descriptions.

## 2. System hierarchy and connections

The hierarchy below groups equipment by function. It is a synthesis of the sources, not an original AECL equipment-classification schedule. Later equipment is included with its date or source qualification.

**WR-1 research reactor**

1. **Reactor and core structures**

   - Calandria vessel, core space, dump ring and lower dump space
   - Aluminum calandria tubes and CO₂ annuli
   - Removable pressure tubes, end connections and channel closures
   - Fuel bundles, central supports, centering rings and experimental inserts

2. **Main heat transport**

   - Organic circuits A and B; circuit C added in 1972

     - Pump/flywheel → inlet headers/feeders → fuel → outlet feeders → exchanger

   - Channel flow restrictions and mechanical connections
   - River/process-water heat rejection and standby cooling water

3. **Organic coolant management**

   - Pressurizing pumps; degassing/expansion/reserve tanks
   - Off-gas condensers, volatile recovery and gas handling
   - Vacuum distillation, makeup and high-boiler removal
   - Side-stream coolers, clay adsorption columns and backup filters
   - Sampling/chemical control; organic supply, nitrogen and relief exhaust

4. **Moderator, reactivity control and shutdown**

   - Heavy-water circulation, heat exchanger and dump tank
   - Helium blowers, regulating valves, accumulator and dump valves
   - Boron addition/removal and moderator purification
   - Neutron instrumentation, regulating circuits and protective trips

5. **Protection and essential services**

   - Pump rundown; emergency organic injection; standby coolers/water
   - Failed-fuel, annulus-leak, area-radiation and ventilation monitoring
   - Fire detection, water sprays, selected-space CO₂ inerting and drainage
   - Normal/standby AC, diesel generation, rectifiers, batteries and instrument air

6. **Shielding and structural cooling**

   - Radial/axial thermal shields and separate cooling circuits
   - Heavy-concrete biological shielding and concrete cooling
   - Rotating top deck plates, lower access and shielded penetrations

7. **Fuel handling and examination**

   - Shutdown refuelling, crane and shielded transfer flasks
   - Organic-filled storage tubes/cans within water-cooled storage facilities
   - Fuel washing, bundle shuffling and transfer to hot cells

8. **Research facilities and waste services**

   - Independent in-reactor loops, including water loop 1L2
   - Organic fast-neutron loops 1L4/1L5/1L6; external hydraulic loop 1L1
   - Process drainage, organic traps, active sumps and liquid-waste treatment

The circuit division let WR-1 test different organic compositions or conditions in the same reactor. A fuel-channel position could instead be connected to an experimental loop. This flexibility also created configuration-specific safety responsibilities: the main organic circuits, water loop and fast-neutron loops did not all have identical equipment or limits. The early main circulating pumps were **1,000 hp (approximately 746 kW), 3,600 rpm** Bingham units. They operated continuously without a standby main pump; a complete spare impeller/shaft/seal assembly was kept available. The flywheel, standby cooling and emergency injection served different functions from a redundant full-duty circulating pump. [R5, §8, p. 9](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=14).

## 3. Dimensions and what happens close to the core

### 3.1 The physical arrangement

**WR-1's hot, pressurized organic coolant was confined to individual fuel channels.** The surrounding heavy water occupied the calandria and provided the main moderator and reflector. The pressure tube and calandria tube were separate structures, with an insulating, monitored CO₂ space between them. [R8, p. 3-5](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=145).

| From a fuel element outward | Function and local concern |
| --- | --- |
| UO₂ or, later, UC fuel | Fission heat must pass through the fuel and fuel-to-clad gap. Fuel composition changes conductivity, temperature, swelling and fission-gas behaviour. |
| Zirconium-alloy sheath; SAP in selected tests | Retains fuel/fission products and transfers heat to organic. Hydrogen uptake, deposits and local cooling affect its performance. |
| Organic-filled passages between elements and around the bundle | Carry the main upward coolant flow. Average channel velocity does not establish the minimum velocity in every subchannel. |
| Pressure tube | Contains the organic pressure boundary. Materials and thickness changed as part of the irradiation programme. |
| CO₂ annulus | Thermally separates hot pressure tube from the calandria tube; monitored gas helps identify organic or heavy-water leakage. It is not the normal fuel-cooling passage. |
| Aluminum calandria tube | Separates the gas space from the heavy-water moderator. |
| Heavy-water moderator/reflector | Moderates neutrons and removes heat deposited in the vessel and tubes; changing its level regulates reactivity. |
| Calandria, thermal shields and biological shield | Contain the moderator region, remove structural heating and attenuate radiation outside the reactor. |

Sources: [R3, §§3–4](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=6); [R4, §5.3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=15); [R7, §§2.2–2.6](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=8); [R8, p. 3-5](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=145).

These temperatures and burnups are **design parameters and targets**, not measurements establishing qualification of all four fuel types. The nominal heat-rating entries, Q/4π = 40 or 44 W/cm, correspond to approximately 50.3 or 55.3 kW/m if Q denotes linear heat rate; that conversion is consistent with the surface-flux and diameter values.

The 1965 preliminary pellet diameter, 0.55 in or approximately 14.0 mm, should not be combined uncritically with the 1966 cladding dimensions. The latter imply a 13.83-mm bore for the Zr–Nb design, before subtracting diametral clearance. Full manufacturing drawings would be needed to reconcile that difference. Fuel-element pitch, wire diameter/lead, hanger dimensions and bundle-to-tube clearance were not established for a common dated driver configuration. Later UC operation cannot be represented simply by changing “UO₂” to “UC” in this table. AECL's 1980 review identifies a **14-element WR-1 driver bundle**. It reports more than 400 UC bundles irradiated and two defects at that reporting date, following conversion beginning in 1973. CNL describes later driver enrichments of 1.33% in selected central sites and 1.95% elsewhere. The early 18-element dimensions above are therefore not a lifetime geometry specification. [R6, pp. 46–48, 59](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=53); [R7, §2.7](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=10).

Source: [R1, Table 1, p. 3/PDF p. 6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4891392#page=6).

Three coupled effects matter particularly close to the fuel:

- **Local flow and deposits:** WR-1 tests at approximately 3.0 and 4.8 m/s average velocity developed increased fouling and some premature fuel failures. Long operation at 7.5 m/s had not shown increased deposits. AECL judged velocities below 5 m/s unacceptable for the tested standard cluster with uncertain subchannel velocities. This is geometry-specific experience, not a universal organic-coolant velocity criterion. [R6, p. 51](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=58).
- **Hydrogen uptake and temperature gradients:** coolant chemistry maintained the zirconium oxide barrier. Cooler wire wraps acted as hydrogen sinks; the 1967 account describes wires approximately 30°C cooler than the sheath. Hydrogen migration to endplates subsequently caused embrittlement and breakage in some bundles, prompting design changes. The destination of hydrogen mattered as well as total uptake. [R4, §4.1, p. 9](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=13); [R6, p. 47](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=54).
- **A cladding defect changes the chemistry inside the element:** organic entering a failed element can decompose and supply hydrogen, causing internal hydriding and progressive deterioration. Failed-fuel monitoring therefore served both contamination control and preservation of removable fuel geometry. The UC conversion required detector improvements because its fission-gas signals were smaller. [R6, pp. 47–48](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=54).

Flow distribution also depended on the loading pattern. The later plant description states that temporarily unfuelled positions received flow restrictors to reproduce the flow of fuelled sites. An empty channel could therefore not simply be treated as an unrestricted parallel path when reconstructing the core hydraulics. [R7, §2.7](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=10).

## 4. What organic cooling required

HB-40, later called OS-84, was a partially hydrogenated terphenyl mixture. Although Santowax OM was the original reference coolant, HB-40 was selected for startup and retained in operation. Its ability to remain liquid at room temperature avoided much of the difficult trace heating associated with solid terphenyl coolant, especially inside narrow core passages. Some trace heating remained necessary, notably at standby-pump seals. [R3, §2](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=2); [R4, §2.6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=10); [R5, §7](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=13).

| Requirement | WR-1 implementation and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Control radiation/thermal decomposition | Degassing removed gases and volatile products; distillation/feed-and-bleed controlled high boilers. Coolant was an evolving mixture rather than a fixed pure-fluid property set. |
| Maintain flow at low temperature | CNL reports a maximum 33% high-boiler concentration because larger concentrations caused viscosity/flow difficulties during shutdown and cold operation. |
| Control fouling | Low chlorine, low metallic oxides, limited oxygen ingress, filtration and clay treatment. The 1967 practice specified chlorine ≤0.5 ppm and water 100–200 ppm; the 1969 account discussed 50–500 ppm water. These are dated practices, not a reconciled chemistry specification. |
| Protect zirconium alloys | A deliberately small water content helped maintain the protective oxide; chlorinated contamination could accelerate hydriding. “Drier is always better” would be an incorrect rule for these materials. |
| Treat side streams | Each later circuit had a degassing tank, two pressurizing pumps, condensers/recycle equipment and particulate treatment. Approximately 15% of **degassing return flow**, not primary flow, passed through the particulate branch, cooled to 300°C before clay adsorption. |
| Exclude oxygen under vacuum | Air ingress through purification-system valve packing and pump seals required bellows-seal valves, better seals and welded connections during commissioning. |
| Limit hot leaks and ignition | Water sprays, leak/smoke detection, ventilation, grounding, explosion-proof fittings, suitable insulation and CO₂ protection of selected hot spaces. |
| Handle spent fuel without exposing it directly to bay water | Organic-filled tubes/cans were immersed in water-cooled storage facilities. This was especially consequential for later water-reactive UC fuel. |
| Manage contaminated organic and gas streams | Condensers, filters, clay, traps, relief exhaust and separate drainage were part of the plant. Low activity during normal operation did not make these streams intrinsically nonradioactive. |

Sources: [R2, pp. 3–4](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=7); [R3, §§2–3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4528366#page=2); [R4, §§2.4–3.1](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=9); [R5, §§5–8](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=11); [R7, §§2.4–2.8](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=9).

The ancillary plant was appreciable: the later A/B degassing reservoirs were about **1.83 m OD × 3.66 m**, clay columns **508 mm OD × 1.52 m**, and backup filters **337 mm OD × 1.57 m**. C-circuit equipment differed; its degassing reservoir was approximately **2.13 m OD × 3.30 m**. These are equipment dimensions, not calculated usable volumes. [R7, Table 4, p. 53](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=53).

## 5. Operating problems, remedies and limits of the record

### 5.1 Commissioning and equipment

| Problem or observation | Reported response/result | Engineering implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main-pump support springs were designed for an 8,000-lb motor; installed motor weighed 12,000 lb. | Revised springs corrected alignment/vibration; commissioning delayed 28 days. | Pump support and actual installed mass mattered independently of coolant chemistry. |
| Opening a fuel channel admitted air and partly gas-locked pumps. | Piping was modified to prevent unstable channel levels and overflow. | Refuelling altered the hydraulic boundary and introduced oxygen. |
| Clay baskets floated during valving-in and released clay. | Top screens and hold-down springs added; filters removed the material before fuel installation. | Cleanup equipment itself could introduce particles. |
| Early pump seals failed due to construction debris. | Subsequent service improved after cleanup; the 1969 account reports preventive seal changes after 12,000–14,000 operating hours. | Clean construction was a significant operating requirement. |
| Crane wiring induced false neutron-rate trips; other electronics suffered cross-talk and temperature sensitivity. | Ion-chamber cables rerouted into conduit; filtering, better components and heat sinks added. | Protection performance required electrical integration checks. |
| Thermal-power computers did not meet performance requirements during commissioning. | Operation used neutron-power control. | An installed instrument was not automatically a demonstrated control capability. |
| Leaking pressure-tube weld and rolled joint in the early record. | CO₂ annulus monitoring located leaks; tube replacement used feeder freeze plugs. R4 reports about 12 hours per tube including fuel handling. | Channel replacement was practical; this does not establish an accident-analysis bound for all tube ruptures. |
| Main heat-exchanger internal leaks in 1978. | R6 attributes them to secondary-water corrosion; inspected organic-side tubes showed no comparable pitting or fouling film. | Secondary-system degradation remained important despite benign primary-side experience. |

Sources: [R2, §§2, 6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=6); [R4, §§5.2–5.3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=14); [R5, §8](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=14); [R6, pp. 36–37](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=43).

### 5.3 Fuel failures and loss of the low-radiation advantage

The 1969 report describes an experimental uranium-impregnated graphite assembly in which eight elements ruptured. Released fission gases and Ba/La-140 contaminated a circuit; piping fields remained as high as **50 R/h** after gas purging. The high background disabled useful failed-fuel detection. Particulate removal and decay allowed return to service in about **six weeks**. Those conditions differ sharply from the same report's normal outlet-feeder fields below **5 mR/h**. Historical exposure-rate units are preserved here. [R5, pp. 10–11, failure](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=15); [R5, p. 6, normal fields](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=11).

The lifetime perspective is less benign than the early operating summaries. CNL's 2020 review states that there were approximately **150 documented fuel failures between 1966 and 1983**, principally associated with experimental fuels at severe conditions but also including driver failures. It describes episodes with piping fields of at least 25 R/h and retained contamination in piping, tanks and other components. That count is not a commercial-fuel failure rate: the population, exposure denominator and experimental duty differ, and “failures” should not be read as 150 independent major accidents. It also does not contradict the narrower report of two UC-driver defects among more than 400 bundles at the earlier review date. [R7, §§6.3.1–6.3.2, pp. 22–23](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=22); [R6, p. 46](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=53).

## 6. Historical safety provisions and their limits

The recovered sources support a description of the safety functions and commissioning evidence. **They do not constitute the complete original operating safety case.** In particular, a shutdown mechanism, emergency tank or successful incident response does not by itself establish margins for every postulated accident.

| Safety function | WR-1 provision/evidence | What remains unestablished here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regulate reactivity | Helium differential pressure supported and controlled heavy-water level; boron concentration was adjusted through addition and ion exchange. | Full control logic, setpoints and configuration history. |
| Shut down the chain reaction | Rapid moderator dump from the core into the lower dump space and dump tank. Later inventory lists six helium-system reactor dump valves. | Full shutdown reliability, independence and accident-specific insertion requirements. |
| Maintain heat removal after a trip/loss of drive | Main pumps had one-ton flywheels for rundown; standby cooling equipment and water were provided. | Coastdown curves, cooling duration and complete loss-of-power performance. |
| Replace lost primary inventory | Two pressurized fresh-organic tanks: PO-TK1 connected to inlet headers/experimental facilities; PO-TK3 to outlet feeders through activity-monitoring lines. | Tank inventories, pressures, actuation details, injection rates and credited duration. |
| Detect fuel/channel degradation | Coolant bleed activity monitoring and CO₂ annulus leak monitoring. | Detection coverage under all conditions; the major fuel-failure event demonstrated a high-background limitation. |
| Control radiation and releases | Fuel cladding, pressure boundaries, shielding, ventilation monitoring and waste/drain systems. | A verified containment leakage specification and complete accident source-term/dose analysis. |
| Control organic fires | Water spray/sprinkler protection, selected-space CO₂ systems, ignition-source control, insulation design and prompt leak response. | A complete operating fire-hazards analysis, including common-cause effects on shutdown and cooling equipment. |
| Supply essential services | Standby diesel generation, rectifiers and battery-backed DC are identified in the retrospective equipment description. | Historical load allocation, qualification and mission times. |

Sources: [R2, §§2.1–2.3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=6); [R5, §§1, 5, 8–10](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=6); [R7, §§2.5–2.6 and Table 4](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=9); [R8, pp. 3-15–3-16](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146078E.pdf#page=155).

### 6.1 Reactivity feedback was not uniformly negative

| Commissioning parameter | Prediction | Measurement |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Clean critical moderator level, 19-site core | 78.2 in | 84.45 in |
| Clean critical moderator level, 25-site core | 57.0 in | 59.5 in |
| Moderator-temperature coefficient | −0.16 mk/°C at 54°C | +0.135 at 10°C; zero at 50°C; −0.09 at 80°C, in mk/°C |
| Net power coefficient | −0.098 mk/% | −0.046 mk/% |
| Coolant-temperature coefficient | −0.023 mk/°C | −0.014 mk/°C |
| B-loop coolant-void quantity | +25.0 mk | **+22.5 mk** |
| Reactivity removed in first second of moderator dump | 12.5 mk | **15.5 mk** |

Source: [R2, Table I, p. 5/PDF p. 9](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=9). The source reports the loop-void quantity in mk, without a “per percent void” denominator; it should be treated as a reported loop void worth, not a differential coefficient. The dump entry is the magnitude removed in the first second. These measurements belong to the commissioning configurations, not every later fuel loading.

The weaker-than-predicted negative power coefficient was attributed partly to sheath heating displacing coolant, adding positive reactivity. The moderator coefficient also changed sign with temperature. Thus, a negative measured net power coefficient did not remove the need to assess positive coolant-void reactivity. Nor is comparing 22.5 mk with 15.5 mk alone a transient safety calculation: the voiding history, trip delay and complete shutdown curve matter. [R2, pp. 5–6](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=9).

Commissioning uncovered concrete shutdown/control interactions. Moderator overflow into the helium balance line degraded dump rate, prompting a high-level trip. Rapid moderator cooling contracted the gas above it, raised moderator level and introduced positive reactivity, prompting a moderator/process-water temperature-difference trip. Dump-tank baffles and discharge-pipe modifications prevented entrained helium from gas-locking pumps. Blower speed was reduced from 1,200 to 900 rpm to keep pump-up within the 0.33-mk/s design rate. [R2, pp. 2–3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4513855#page=6).

### 6.2 Organic-specific safety qualifications

Low operating pressure reduced some mechanical and stored-energy burdens, but the coolant remained combustible and its composition evolved. The 1969 account gives autoignition temperatures of approximately 350–395°C depending on high-boiler content; the 1967 account reports 390–425°C for its coolant history. These should not be collapsed into one universal ignition threshold. Hot-surface contact time, surrounding air, absorbed coolant in insulation and mechanical ignition sources all mattered. [R4, §2.3](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=8); [R5, §5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=11).

Organic vapour also affected protection equipment. In 1969, ventilation filters had short service lives in the organic atmosphere; the operating account describes normal bypass with automatic insertion on abnormal activity and notes unresolved safety concerns. This was an operating workaround under development, not evidence of an intrinsically complete filtered-confinement solution. [R5, §7](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=13).

For later UC fuel, water contact introduced additional chemistry: UC can hydrolyze and generate combustible gases, while an organic-wetted fuel-handling route avoids direct routine exposure to bay water. Accident-release and moderator-as-backup-heat-sink calculations in the **proposed OCR-500** must not be transferred to WR-1 without its own analysis. The same applies to that proposal's two poison shutdown systems and Halon containment protection; the recovered WR-1 evidence instead describes moderator dumping and selected-space CO₂ protection. [R6, §§2.4.2, 3.3.3](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=36); [R7, §2.8](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80124/146112E.pdf#page=10).

## 7. Economics: observed benefits and projected costs

**No complete WR-1 construction-cost or annual operating-cost account was established from these sources.** Because WR-1 had no turbine, it has no observed electricity cost or measured electrical efficiency. The 37–39% efficiency discussed in the 1969 paper was an expectation for a power reactor using similar outlet conditions. [R5, pp. 1, 11](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=6).

| Economic evidence | What it supports |
| --- | --- |
| Above-85% early availability; long pump-seal intervals; accessible equipment under ordinary radiation conditions | Operating practicality and potential maintenance savings. No quantified lifetime cost advantage follows. |
| Fewer than 5% of approximately 850 valves reportedly required new packing/gaskets in over 13 years | A useful component-service observation, subject to the particular equipment and maintenance programme. |
| UO₂ chosen initially because existing CANDU manufacturing methods served small WR-1 production lots | Research-reactor fuel selection included fabrication cost and neutron-production needs, not only the best fuel for a large organic power station. |
| Coolant replacement, chemical analysis, filters/clay, waste handling, fire protection and failure-related outages | Recurring costs that must accompany claims of simpler low-pressure equipment. The report does not assign unreported WR-1 expenditures. |

Sources: [R4, §§4.1, 5](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4557804#page=12); [R5, §§4, 8–9](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/4114552#page=10); [R6, §3.2.5](https://inis.iaea.org/records/e4zqc-d2129/files/11559681.pdf#page=43).

## 8. Evidence limits and engineering assessment

| Open item | Consequence for use of this report |
| --- | --- |
| Complete original hazards report, addenda, design manuals and WR-1 Handbook were not recovered for this review. | Safety functions are described; accident acceptance, setpoints and full system reliability are not independently established. |
| Vessel diameter, later tube counts and some fuel dimensions conflict across sources. | Use the dated geometry tables for understanding, not as a reconciled as-built model. |
| No complete driver drawing set, channel flow map, pump curve or heat-exchanger performance set was established. | A defensible subchannel or transient model would need additional inputs; source temperatures alone cannot provide its margins. |
| This review performs unit conversions and geometry/accounting checks only. | No new thermal-hydraulic, neutronic or accident simulation was performed. |
| Early successful operation, later deliberate experiments and lifetime contamination have different scopes. | Do not extrapolate early availability or low radiation fields to every later operating condition. |
| Some converted documents remain under review. | Consequential tables and passages used here were checked against retained originals; this report does not certify the entire converted corpus. |
| Original incident records and detailed WR-1 financial accounts remain missing. | The incident chronology is selective, and economic conclusions remain qualitative or explicitly prospective. |

WR-1's strongest demonstrated result was sustained high-temperature organic cooling in replaceable pressure-tube channels. The same operating record shows why coolant chemistry, subchannel flow, hydrogen management, leak/fire control and failed-fuel detection were central reactor systems. Those requirements, together with moderator-level shutdown and configuration-specific protection, are the most useful lessons from the region close to its core.
